## 1–4. Public Website: Structure, Positioning, Portfolio, Project 000

### Segmentation — Celebrations / Business & Brand / Custom Systems
**[REC]** Keep the three lanes, but **don't give them equal visual weight at launch.** Custom Systems should be a **quiet capability, not a headline lane** — reasons:
- You have zero case studies for it yet, and a prospect landing on "we build booking systems and dashboards" with nothing to show is worse than not mentioning it prominently.
- It's your highest-value, lowest-volume offer — it should be discovered by people who ask "can you also build X," not be the first thing a café owner sees and bounces from.
**[REC]** Structure: **Celebrations** and **Business & Brand** as the two primary nav items/homepage lanes (matches your actual initial customer segments from Phase 1). **Custom Systems** appears as a single line on the Services page ("Need something more — booking, dashboards, logins, payments? Talk to us.") and gets its own page only once you have 1–2 real examples to show.

### Homepage
**[REC]** Order: (1) one sharp positioning line + subline, no stock-photo hero — a real screenshot or live embedded demo of your best concept project instead; (2) the two primary lanes as visual cards; (3) 3–4 portfolio highlights; (4) a one-line process/trust strip ("Design → Build → Launch, most projects in under 2 weeks"); (5) simple pricing-from indicators, not full price list; (6) inquiry CTA. Keep it to one scroll of substance — resist the urge to prove everything on the homepage; that's what the portfolio and process pages are for.

### Pricing presentation
**[REC]** Show **"starting from ₹X"** per category on the Services page (matching Phase 1's introductory tier), not a full rate card — a full public rate card invites page-count-style negotiation (the exact problem Phase 1's Addendum Section 9 solved internally) and doesn't let your internal scoring model do its job. Custom Systems shows no public price at all — "scoped individually."

### Process page
**[REC]** Show your real flow simplified to client-facing language (Section 12 below has the internal version): Discover → Design → Build → Launch → (optional) Grow. Include realistic timelines per lane (Celebrations: 3–7 days; Business & Brand: 1–3 weeks; Custom Systems: scoped).

### Team page
**[REC]** Show all three founders as real people with real names, faces, and specific roles — "three engineering/design students building Algoryxz" is honest and works *in your favor* for local/college-network trust; don't try to hide the "students" fact, frame it as energy/technical currency instead ("we build the way modern software actually gets built now — AI-accelerated, fast iteration — because that's how we learned").

### Trust signals at zero clients
**[REC]** Since you have no client logos yet: lean on (a) transparent process/timeline commitments, (b) visible founder identities and real technical depth (a genuinely well-built site *is* the trust signal — Project 000 doing this job is more valuable than any badge), (c) an honest "New Studio" framing with a founding-cohort incentive (Section 5) rather than pretending you're established.

### FAQs, legal/footer
**[REC]** FAQ: revision policy, what's included/excluded per package, timeline, hosting/domain ownership question (ties to Phase 1 Section 14 handover topic — answer honestly: "you own your site; hosting/domain handled by you or via our maintenance plan"), payment schedule. Footer: contact, entity name once incorporated (informal "Algoryxz" is fine pre-incorporation, just don't claim a registered-company status you don't have yet), privacy basics for data you collect via the inquiry form.

### Portfolio with zero/few real clients
**[REC]**
- **How many concept projects:** 4–6 at launch — 2 Celebrations, 2 Business & Brand, 1 optional Custom Systems teaser (even a non-live prototype/video walkthrough), plus Algoryxz itself as Project 000.
- **Full live site vs. case study only:** Celebrations concepts should be **fully live, interactive** (they're cheap/fast to build and "click around a real wedding site" converts far better than screenshots for this category — it's an experiential product). Business & Brand concepts can be **case-study format with a live demo link** rather than needing to look like a real ongoing business.
- **Fictional brands:** **[REC]** acceptable and normal for concept work — the honesty issue is disclosure, not fictionality.
- **Disclosure:** label clearly but not apologetically — a small "Concept Project" tag on the card is enough; don't bury it in fine print (that reads as deceptive if discovered) and don't over-explain it either (that reads as insecure). One line: *"Concept project — built to demonstrate our process, not a real client."*
- **Show process/iterations:** yes, briefly, for 1–2 of the deeper case studies — a "before/after" or 2–3 design-iteration snapshot is a strong differentiator against agencies who only show final polish; it's also relatively cheap to produce since you're documenting your own real process anyway.
- **Show code/technical details:** only on the Custom Systems teaser and only at a light level (architecture diagram, not raw code) — code depth belongs in a technical blog post or GitHub, not the sales portfolio.
- **Interactive demos vs. static screenshots:** interactive wins decisively for Celebrations (it's the whole product); for Business & Brand, a well-shot screenshot sequence + one live link is enough — don't over-invest interactivity budget on the lower-differentiation category.

### Algoryxz itself — Project 000
**[REC]** Should demonstrate: strong typography/visual design, real responsiveness (test on actual phones, not just resize), one or two tasteful motion/interaction details (not motion-for-its-own-sake), fast load (this is a measurable, checkable claim — run Lighthouse and don't ship if it's mediocre), and basic accessibility (contrast, alt text, keyboard nav) since it's cheap to get right and a technical prospect might actually check.
**[REC] Should NOT:** sacrifice usability for cleverness (no obscure custom-scroll navigation that confuses first-time visitors), take more than ~3 seconds to load meaningfully, or bury the inquiry CTA under design flourish. **The studio site is a sales tool first, an art piece second** — best-practice creative-studio sites (the pattern across successful small studios, not any one you should copy) balance a strong opening visual moment with a fast, obvious path to "see work" and "get in touch" within one or two clicks.

## 2. Positioning / Copy

**[REC]** The strategic pattern behind good small-studio positioning (observed across the category, not copied wording): **specific capability + specific outcome + implicit contrast**, never abstract adjectives. "Cutting-edge" and "innovative" fail because they're unfalsifiable — nobody can picture what they mean. Strong positioning names *what you actually do differently* in concrete terms a prospect can picture.

**[REC] Directional positioning for Algoryxz** (not final copy, a strategic direction for Deeptiman/copywriting to execute):
- Core angle: **"A small studio, built like a modern engineering team."** You genuinely are fast because of your workflow — say what that means in outcomes, not in the word "AI": *"design and build, done in days not months"* — true, checkable, differentiating.
- Against Wix/Framer/Squarespace/Shopify: *you're not fighting the DIY tool, you're the option for people who don't want to DIY it* — "you could build it yourself on Wix over a weekend, or we build it properly while you run your business."
- Against Fiverr/cheap freelancers: *design + engineering as a real team*, not one overloaded person — this is your most defensible structural difference and it's true.
- Against a normal agency: *you get the founders, not a project manager relaying to a junior* — small-studio directness is a real, common advantage worth naming.
- Against "just use Claude/Codex themselves": this is your most interesting competitive question and worth answering honestly rather than dodging — the answer is **you're not selling the AI, you're selling the judgment, design taste, and finished product**; a business owner *could* prompt their way to a site, the way they could also build their own accounting spreadsheet instead of hiring a bookkeeper — most don't, because the value isn't typing the thing, it's knowing what the right thing to build and design is, and standing behind it afterward.

## 5. First 3 Clients — Execution Playbook

**[REC]** Do not treat this as generic "network more." Concrete sequence:

1. **Identify 10–15 specific real prospects from your existing personal orbit** before building anything else — actual named people: a café your family/friends frequent, a photographer a friend follows, a salon someone's parent goes to, a friend's small side-business. Personal/warm contacts only for the first 3 — cold outreach starts at "First 10."
2. **Contact method:** in-person or a warm personal WhatsApp/DM from the founder who actually knows them — never a cold form-fill link for these first three. A physical visit works best for cafés/salons/shops (you can see their current online presence or lack of it on the spot); DM works for photographers/creators.
3. **What to say — the actual pitch, not "we do websites":** lead with something *specific you noticed about them* — "I noticed [business] doesn't have a website / your Instagram bio just says 'DM to order' — we build sites for local businesses like yours, want to see an example of what we could do?" **Specificity beats "we're a new studio" every time.**
4. **Show a mockup or not?** **[REC]** For the first 3, offer a **free, small, time-boxed mockup for the specific prospect** (a single homepage concept, 2–3 hours of work max, not a full build) — this is the one place in your whole funnel where doing unpaid work up front is justified, precisely because you have zero portfolio credibility yet and it's the fastest way to earn trust. **Do not do this past the first 3–5 clients** — once you have a real portfolio, point to that instead (Section 6).
5. **Avoiding free speculative work becoming a habit:** cap it explicitly — "one small mockup, free, to show you what we'd do" — and if they want changes to the mockup before committing to pay, that's the signal to stop and ask for the deposit instead.
6. **Introductory discount:** yes, explicitly framed as a **"founding client" rate** (ties to Phase 1's introductory pricing tier) — *"as one of our first clients, you get [specific discount] and we'll feature the project in our portfolio."* The trade for the discount is real: portfolio rights and a testimonial ask.
7. **Qualification for these first 3 specifically:** almost none needed — the goal here is proof-of-concept and portfolio, not profit. The only real filter: will they actually pay something (even discounted) and let you show the work? A friend who wants it entirely free with no portfolio credit isn't a client, that's charity — fine to do occasionally but don't count it toward your "first 3."

## 7. First 25 Clients — What Changes

**[REC]** Once you have real work, testimonials, and 1–2 recurring clients, the shift is from **founder-time-intensive outreach to leverage-based channels**:
- **Formal referral system:** ask every satisfied client directly, and consider a modest referral incentive for *other businesses/professionals* (not clients) who send leads — Section 17 has norms.
- **Local SEO:** a genuinely well-built site (which you already have to produce anyway) plus Google Business Profile optimization for Algoryxz itself starts compounding here; not worth investing in earlier when you have no content/case-study depth to rank.
- **Niche landing pages:** one dedicated page per lane ("Wedding websites in Bhubaneswar," "Café & restaurant websites Odisha") once you have 2–3 real examples per niche — this is a cheap, high-leverage move once the content exists to support it.
- **Case-study marketing:** start actively publishing (not just hosting) case studies once you have 3–5 real ones — share on the founders' own social profiles, tag clients (with permission).
- **Limited paid acquisition:** only worth testing once organic/referral channels show you a real cost-per-lead baseline to compare against — small, capped budget tests, not a scaled spend.
- **CRM/sales automation:** a shared spreadsheet is still probably fine through 25 clients; a lightweight free-tier CRM (Zoho CRM free tier, or a Notion-based pipeline) becomes worth the setup time once lead volume makes the spreadsheet genuinely hard to track — don't build/buy this preemptively.
**[REC]** You stop relying *primarily* on personal networks once referrals + local SEO + niche pages are generating leads without a founder having to personally know the prospect — typically somewhere around the 15–25 client mark if the above is executed, not a fixed date.

## 8. Client Qualification

**[REC] Lightweight intake, form-first:** a short website form (5–7 fields) filters obvious mismatches before a call is spent — **not** a WhatsApp-first flow for unknown leads, since that invites open-ended scope discussion before you're ready to price it. WhatsApp/call is fine as the *follow-up* channel once the form is qualified.

**[REC] Form fields (keep to under 2 minutes to complete):** name/business, what they need (dropdown: Celebration site / Business site / Something custom), rough timeline, rough budget band (give 3–4 ranges, don't leave it open-text), do they have existing content/photos/logo (yes/no/partial), how did they hear about you.

**[REC] Red flags to reject or deprioritize:** no budget range selected or picks the lowest band while describing Custom-Systems-level scope; "I'll know it when I see it" with no reference points when asked for examples they like; wants unlimited revisions or free ongoing changes as a condition of starting; unclear decision-maker ("I need to check with my partner/team" repeated across multiple touches without progress); urgency mismatched with budget ("need it tomorrow" + lowest tier).
**[REC] When to reject outright:** budget band is below your Phase 1 introductory floor for the described scope, or the prospect wants a "sample" before any commitment at all (that's speculative free work outside the founding-client exception in Section 5).

## 9. Sales Call / Discovery — 20–30 Minute Framework

**[REC] Structure:**
1. **(5 min) Business goals first, not features** — "What's the site supposed to *do* for your business?" (get bookings, look credible, replace Instagram, sell products) before any feature talk.
2. **(5–8 min) Current state** — what exists today (nothing / Instagram-only / an old broken site), what they like/dislike about competitors' sites (gives you real design reference points).
3. **(5 min) Scope discovery** — walk through your fixed package options relevant to their category; let them pick a starting point rather than free-listing every feature they can imagine.
4. **(3–5 min) Budget and timeline confirmation** — restate the band from their intake form, confirm it still holds now that scope is clearer.
5. **(3–5 min) Explain price and process** — give a range or a specific number if scope is clear enough (using the Phase 1 Addendum scoring model internally, not shown to them), explain the payment schedule (Section 11) and timeline.
6. **Close or next step** — if scope and budget align, send the proposal/quote within 24–48 hours (strike while warm); if not aligned, say so honestly rather than quoting low to "figure it out later."
**[REC] When NOT to take the project:** budget genuinely below what the described scope requires and they won't move; they push back hard on the 2-included-revisions policy before work even starts (predicts scope-creep pain); they want to skip the deposit (Section 11) "just this once."

## 11. Payment Structure

**[FACT]** No single documented "Indian small-business standard" exists for web-design deposits, but milestone/deposit-based payment is the norm across the studios and freelance-pricing sources reviewed in Phase 1 — the practical question is scaling the split to project size and risk.

**[REC]**
- **₹2–5k projects:** **100% upfront.** At this size, chasing a partial payment costs more founder time than the risk is worth, and these are typically fast-turnaround Celebrations work anyway.
- **₹5–15k:** **50% upfront / 50% before final delivery/launch.**
- **₹15–50k:** **40/30/30** — 40% upfront, 30% at design approval (a real milestone gate), 30% before launch.
- **₹50k+ custom work:** **milestone-based**, tied to your internal Section 10 milestones (discovery/scope sign-off, design approval, dev-complete/QA, launch) — no single large payment gate; this protects both sides on longer builds.
**[REC] Work starts only after the first payment clears** — no exceptions, stated plainly in the quote. **If a client disappears mid-project:** work pauses at the last completed milestone; the deposit is non-refundable past the point work has started (state this upfront in the quote/SOW, not as a surprise later); if they return, work resumes from where it paused, re-quoted if enough time has passed that scope/pricing may have shifted.

## 12. Contract / SOW Flow — Weighted by Project Size

**[REC]** The full Inquiry → Discovery → Quote → SOW → Contract → Deposit → Work sequence is correctly heavy for Business & Brand and Custom Systems, and **too heavy for a ₹2,500 personal project** — a proposal-site client isn't going to sign a formal contract for a same-week deliverable.

**[REC] Two paths:**
- **Light path (Celebrations, under ~₹5k):** Inquiry (form) → package selection → payment (100% upfront, Section 11) → a **single confirmation message** restating what's included, revision policy, and delivery timeline (this can be a templated WhatsApp/email message, not a legal contract) → work begins.
- **Full path (Business & Brand, Custom Systems):** the complete sequence as described, with a real (even if simple, templated) SOW document and contract once you're incorporated.
**[REC] What must be documented even on the lightest/tiniest jobs, no exceptions:** what's included (feature list), what's excluded, number of revisions, price, payment status, delivery timeline. This can live in one short written message, but it must exist in writing — this is the cheapest possible insurance against the "invisible requirements" problem flagged in your brief (Section 13 below).

## 13. Revision / Change Request System

**[REC] Definitions, stated plainly to clients:**
- **Revision** = adjusting something already built within the agreed scope (change this photo, make this text bigger, move this section) — covered by the 2 included rounds.
- **New scope** = anything not in the original feature list (add a new page, add a booking system to a site that didn't include one) — quoted and paid separately, work paused until approved.
- **Bug fix** = something built that doesn't work as specified (a broken link, a form that doesn't send) — always free, not counted as a revision round, no time limit within a reasonable post-launch window (tie to the maintenance-plan boundary from Phase 1 — indefinite free bug fixing isn't sustainable past the initial support window).
**[REC] Submission and batching:** revisions submitted through **one channel only** (a simple form or a single running document — e.g., a shared Google Doc list, not scattered WhatsApp messages) and **batched per round**, not trickled in one at a time — state this upfront: "send everything for round 1 together; we'll implement and share round 2 for your final batch of changes."
**[REC] Preventing invisible WhatsApp scope-creep:** every accepted change (revision or new scope) gets logged in the same running document, even if it started as a casual WhatsApp message — "confirming in writing: adding X, this counts as your round 2 / this is additional scope at ₹Y." This single habit is what actually prevents the problem your brief is worried about — not a policy on paper, but the discipline of writing every verbal/chat agreement into the one shared record.

## 14. Client Content/Asset Collection

**[REC]** Use a **single shared Google Drive folder per project**, created at kickoff, with a clearly named subfolder structure (Logo / Photos / Copy / Menu-or-Pricing / Other) — simplest, zero-cost, universally familiar to non-technical Indian small-business clients (vs. Notion or a custom portal, which adds friction for someone who just wants to drop photos from their phone).
**[REC] Deadline/content policy, stated at kickoff:** a specific date by which content is needed to hit the agreed timeline; if content is late, **the delivery date moves accordingly, not your other commitments** — state this plainly so a late client doesn't create a project pile-up. For Celebrations work, offer to source stock/template content as a paid add-on for clients with zero assets, rather than letting the project stall indefinitely.

## 15. Proposal/Wedding/Personal Client Flow

**[REC]** This should be the most productized, lowest-friction flow in the whole business — it's your fastest-selling category and the one most naturally suited to a self-serve funnel later:
- **Discovery:** social/portfolio-driven — they see a live example and want something similar.
- **Package selection:** Good/Better/Best tiers (Section 10), chosen directly, no discovery call needed for the base tiers.
- **Info/photo submission:** a simple form (names, date, event details, RSVP needs) + the Drive folder (Section 14).
- **Personalization:** template-based design system with real customization within it (color, layout variant, content) — this is where your AI-assisted production genuinely shines for speed without sacrificing a personal feel.
- **Payment:** 100% upfront (Section 11).
- **Preview:** a shareable private preview link before final "go live."
- **Revision:** 1–2 rounds, tightly time-boxed (24–48 hour turnaround given the usually time-sensitive nature — a wedding date doesn't move).
- **Delivery/hosting duration:** **[REC]** default to a defined hosting period (e.g., 1 year included, matches how SitesPlaced-style competitors price) rather than indefinite free hosting — renewal becomes a natural, low-friction upsell into a light "Care" plan.
- **Privacy/deletion:** offer a clear post-event deletion option (especially relevant given guest data/photos) — this is good practice regardless of formal DPDP Act obligations (flagged for Phase 3/legal research) and a genuine trust signal for a category built on personal/sensitive content.
**[REC]** This flow is a strong candidate to become a fully self-serve productized funnel later (pick template → customize → pay → done, no founder time per project) — worth flagging now as a Phase 4-adjacent idea, but not something to build before you have real volume proving the demand pattern (10+ real Celebrations projects).

## 16. Local Business Client Flow

**[REC] What actually sells to this segment** — lead with the *specific gap*, not a generic pitch:
- No website at all, Instagram-only presence with "DM to order"
- Broken or unclaimed **Google Business Profile** listing (wrong hours, no photos, no website link) — genuinely common and easy to point out concretely
- Menu/services only exist as an Instagram post or a WhatsApp-forwarded PDF
- Poor mobile experience on whatever site they do have
- No way to capture a lead (no contact form, phone-only)

**[REC] When NOT to sell a website:** if a prospect's actual problem is a broken/unclaimed Google Business Profile or a disorganized Instagram, **fix or recommend fixing that first** (sometimes for free or as part of the pitch), rather than selling a full site that doesn't address their real bottleneck. A café with great foot traffic and zero online discovery needs Google Business Profile optimization more urgently than a full custom website — leading with an honest diagnosis, even when it doesn't immediately sell your biggest package, is what makes a first-time local pitch credible and referral-worthy.

## 17. Partnerships

**[REC] Best-fit partner types for your specific categories:** wedding planners and photographers (feed your Celebrations lane directly — highest-intent referral source you have access to), graphic/social-media/print-shop vendors (feed Business & Brand — they already work with local businesses that need a web presence they don't offer), college event organizers (feed the funded-fest segment from Phase 1 Addendum #8).
**[FACT]** Referral fee norms for design/creative-services referrals generally run **5–15% of the project fee** for one-off projects (higher for warm/qualified introductions, lower for cold ones); overly generous rates (20%+) are used by some agencies specifically to actively recruit referral partners as a channel, not typical for passive relationship-based referrals.
**[REC]** Start with **10% of the project fee, one-time, paid after the client's first payment clears** — simple, matches market norms, easy to explain. Don't overcomplicate with recurring-revenue-share referral terms at this stage.
**[REC] White-label/overflow work for local agencies:** a real, low-risk revenue channel worth pursuing — local agencies (several exist in Bhubaneswar per current search results, mostly WordPress/template-based, none obviously offering AI-accelerated custom builds) sometimes need dev capacity for overflow work; this is worth a direct, professional outreach once you have a portfolio to show, framed as "we can take on overflow build work" rather than positioning yourselves as a competitor to them.
**[REC] Risk:** be explicit in writing about who owns the client relationship and who's liable for what in white-label work — informal handshake overflow-work arrangements are exactly the kind of undocumented arrangement Phase 1's Addendum flagged as risky.

## 18. Testimonials / Social Proof

**[REC]** Ask **every** client for a testimonial at project completion, timed right after delivery (peak satisfaction moment) — make it easy: send 2–3 specific prompt questions rather than "write us a review," e.g. *"What was your main worry before we started, and how do you feel about the site now?"* — specific answers read as more credible than generic praise.
**[REC]** Prioritize, in order of credibility to a new prospect: (1) a **Google review** for Algoryxz itself once you have a business listing (public, verifiable, searchable — the single strongest trust signal for local business prospects specifically), (2) a short **video testimonial** (even a 20-second phone-recorded clip) for 1–2 standout clients, (3) written testimonials with the client's name and business (not anonymous), (4) LinkedIn recommendations from any B2B-leaning clients (startups, funded fest organizers).
**[REC]** Never fabricate testimonials or metrics — beyond the ethical issue, it's also a durable business risk: a small-market studio in one city gets caught on fabricated claims fast, and the reputational cost in a network this size (word-of-mouth is your primary channel) would be disproportionate.

## 19. Case Study Format

**[REC]** What buyers actually care about, in order: **(1) does it look good** (the visual proof), **(2) what specific problem did it solve for a business like mine**, **(3) can I trust this studio to deliver** (process/timeline honesty). Technical stack details rank far below this for a small-business buyer — include them briefly, don't lead with them.

**[REC] Structure (short, scannable, not developer documentation):**
1. One strong hero image/screenshot + client name/category
2. 2–3 sentence problem statement (business language: "no online presence," "losing bookings to a competitor with a better site," not technical language)
3. What you built (bullet list, plain language)
4. 2–3 more screenshots/live-demo link
5. One short testimonial quote
6. Timeline delivered in (a concrete number is a strong trust signal: "delivered in 6 days")
7. Tech stack as a small, optional footer detail, not a section — relevant to a technical prospect evaluating Custom Systems work, irrelevant to everyone else

## 20. Launch Strategy

**[REC] Sequence, not simultaneous:**
1. **Portfolio first, quietly** — build and publish Project 000 + the 4–6 concept projects before any public announcement; a "launch" with an empty portfolio wastes the moment.
2. **Start selling quietly in parallel** — your first 3 clients (Section 5) can and should happen *before* any public launch moment; real client work happening quietly is better proof than an announcement with nothing behind it yet.
3. **Public announcement once you have both a real portfolio and 1–3 real (even small/discounted) client projects** — founder personal social posts (not a faceless brand account first), telling the honest founding story, linking the site.
4. **Limited introductory slots** — announce a capped number of "founding client" discounted slots (ties to Section 5's founding-rate framing) at the public launch moment specifically to create a clear, honest reason to act now rather than "get in touch sometime."
**[REC]** Don't release all concept projects simultaneously as a wall of content — stagger 1–2 per week around the launch moment to sustain visibility across multiple weeks instead of one day's attention spike.

## 23. Validation Criteria

**[ASSUMPTION]** Thresholds are planning targets, not universal benchmarks — calibrate after your first real month tells you your actual close rate and hours-per-project.

**At 30 days — CONTINUE if:** portfolio live, at least 1 paid client secured or in progress, no major founder-conflict or time-capacity crisis. **CHANGE if:** outreach is happening but consistently failing to convert (positioning or pricing likely misaligned — revisit Section 2/copy or Phase 1 pricing before assuming demand is the problem). **STOP/PAUSE if:** genuinely can't sustain outreach effort around coursework at all — a capacity problem, not a demand problem, and worth naming honestly rather than blaming the market.

**At 60 days — CONTINUE if:** 3+ paid clients delivered, at least 1 referral or repeat-interest signal, effective hourly rate (Phase 1 Addendum Section 4 metric) at or above your ~₹400–900/hr baseline. **CHANGE if:** clients are converting but effective hourly rate is consistently low (scope creep or underpricing — tighten the revision/change-request discipline from Section 13 before changing anything else). **STOP/PAUSE if:** fewer than 2 paid clients despite consistent outreach across 60 days — a real demand or positioning signal, not just bad luck.

**At 90 days — CONTINUE if:** 5–10+ clients delivered, at least 1 recurring-plan client signed, referral channel producing real leads without founder-initiated outreach. **CHANGE if:** volume is fine but recurring revenue isn't landing (revisit the Care/Grow/Scale pitch and timing — are you asking at the right moment, right after a good delivery experience?). **STOP/PAUSE if:** consistent client acquisition but consistently unsustainable effective hourly rate even after tightening scope discipline — a pricing-floor problem worth a hard pricing reset rather than continued absorption.

## 24. Competitor / Substitute Research

**[FACT]** Local Bhubaneswar/Odisha web design competitors are numerous and mostly positioned around **generic agency language** ("leading," "best," "professional"), **template/WordPress-based delivery**, and **low headline pricing** — one prominent local competitor advertises sites "from ₹4,999," another cites "13+ years experience," another "500+ happy clients/11+ years." None of the local competitors surfaced in current search results position around AI-accelerated production, a small named-founder team, or a segmented Celebrations/Business/Custom-Systems offering — **this is real, verifiable white space for Algoryxz's positioning**, not an assumption.
**[REC]** This sharpens your offer directly: don't compete on "years of experience" (you have none) or on being the cheapest (established local players already own that ground at ₹4,999 entry points) — compete on **speed with genuine design quality**, **founder-direct access**, and **segmented expertise** (a studio that clearly specializes in occasion sites *and* business sites, rather than a generic "we build any website" shop).
**[FACT]** Global DIY builders (Wix, Squarespace, Framer, Webflow, Shopify) and AI website generators are increasingly commoditizing the "quick site" category at the bottom of the market — reinforcing Phase 1's conclusion that competing there is a losing game; your differentiation has to be in judgment, design taste, and custom capability, not speed-to-publish alone (the DIY tools already win on raw speed-to-publish for the simplest cases).
**[FACT]** Fiverr/gig-marketplace freelancers represent the true price floor (₹3,000–8,000 range per Phase 1 research) — not a segment to chase.
